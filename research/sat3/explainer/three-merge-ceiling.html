Why a third merge doesn't speed up 3-SAT

The new 3-SAT algorithms pay 0.707 of brute force at every level of their recursion. A third "merge" looked like a way to pay only 0.577. Here is why it still costs 0.707.

1The algorithm groups nodes to save work

Picture a grid of nodes with a wire between every pair. Brute force enumerates every wire. A merge groups nodes into clusters, so wires inside a cluster no longer cost anything.

An algebraic identity splits the problem into pieces, and each piece is handled by whichever merge makes it cheapest. The current algorithms use merges 1 and 2.

2One number decides the speed

Call ρ the share of brute-force cost that the worst piece still pays. Lower is faster.

Scale drawn to proportion: 0.577 = 1/√3, 0.707 = 1/√2. Reaching 0.577 would turn the running time from 2^(n/(log n)^0.5) into 2^(n/(log n)^0.79).

3To prove a limit, play the adversary with geometry

Place every cluster as a point (a hub) in some space, and let distance stand for cost. A wire wants to run straight between its two ends. Under a merge it must detour through the hubs of its two clusters.

If you can place the points so the detour is long under every merge at once, no set of pieces can be cheap. The longer the forced detours, the higher the floor under ρ. So the question becomes: how good a placement can the adversary find?

4The right space needs two opposite properties

The adversary's best placement depends on the shape of the space. Only "non-positively curved" spaces are allowed. Three kinds matter.

A building is many flat sheets glued along shared edges: flat inside a sheet, tree-like across sheets. It is a classical object from geometry. Placing each cluster at a point of a finite vector space inside such a building makes every wire's detour equally long under all three merges.

5The floor meets the ceiling

A separate inequality, checked in the Lean proof assistant, says no allowed space can ever force more than 0.707. The building reaches exactly that.

Bar heights are to scale between 0.5 and 0.707. The building's value is 0.707 minus a small term that shrinks as the design grows.

What it means

  • On these designs, a third merge buys nothing: the adversary can force 0.707, the same as two merges.
  • The only escape is if that pricing turns out not to be legitimate. That would prove a major open hypothesis ("contracting") and give a much faster 3-SAT algorithm. So there is no small improvement here: nothing, or a breakthrough.

Limits: this covers designs built from vector spaces over finite fields, one level of the recursion at a time. Designs built from cyclic groups are still open. The "nothing" half assumes the contracting hypothesis is false, as is widely expected. It is not a lower bound for 3-SAT in general. An AI referee checked the result; no human has.
